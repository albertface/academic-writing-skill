# Academic Writing Principles

Use this reference for academic structure, paragraph logic, coherence, transitions, and revision planning.

## Paragraph Structure

Treat a strong academic paragraph as a unit with one controlling purpose.

- Ensure the topic sentence names the topic and limits it with a controlling idea.
- Prefer an early topic sentence for reader guidance, especially in formal academic English.
- Check that the topic sentence is a complete sentence, neither a fragment nor a broad title-like phrase.
- Make the topic sentence general enough to govern the paragraph but specific enough to be developed in one paragraph.
- Use supporting sentences to explain, specify, prove, illustrate, or qualify the topic sentence.
- Use a concluding sentence for stand-alone paragraphs or when a paragraph needs closure; omit routine mini-conclusions inside tightly connected manuscript sections if they make the prose repetitive.

## Unity

Unity means the paragraph develops one main idea from beginning to end.

When polishing, remove, relocate, or flag sentences that:

- introduce a second main topic;
- provide interesting but irrelevant background;
- develop a side issue more than the paragraph's stated claim;
- belong in a different paragraph because they support a different controlling idea.

If a paragraph contains two closely related ideas, keep them together only when they advance the same claim. If they require different evidence or lead to different conclusions, split the paragraph.

## Coherence

Coherence means the reader can follow the relationships among ideas without guessing.

Use four main devices:

- Repeat key nouns or technical terms when needed for clarity; avoid replacing every key term with a synonym if precision suffers.
- Use pronouns consistently and only when the referent is unmistakable.
- Add transition signals to mark sequence, contrast, addition, cause, effect, example, concession, summary, or conclusion.
- Arrange ideas in a logical order: chronological, spatial, general-to-specific, specific-to-general, order of importance, problem-solution, cause-effect, comparison-contrast, or claim-evidence-commentary.

Do not overuse transitions. If every sentence begins with a connector, revise the underlying logic instead of adding more signals.

## Essay And Manuscript Organization

For academic essays and paper sections, check for the same structure at larger scale:

- Introduction: moves from broader context to the specific problem, gap, purpose, or thesis.
- Thesis or central claim: states the paper's controlling idea and, when helpful, previews major divisions.
- Body sections: each paragraph or subsection develops one part of the central claim.
- Conclusion: summarizes main findings or implications, restates the thesis in new words, and avoids introducing unsupported new claims.

For empirical papers, adapt this logic to the local genre: introduction/gap, methods, results, discussion, limitations, and implications.

## Thesis And Topic Sentence Checks

Use these tests during polishing:

- Can the reader identify the main claim after the introduction?
- Does each paragraph have a clear controlling idea?
- Do the body paragraphs follow the order promised by the thesis or section opening?
- Does each topic sentence connect backward to the previous paragraph and forward to the paragraph's evidence?
- Are claims appropriately limited by scope, population, method, or evidence?

## Support And Evidence

Academic support can include facts, examples, quotations, paraphrases, summaries, statistics, methods, results, and logical explanation.

When editing, verify that evidence is not merely inserted but interpreted. A strong evidence sequence usually follows this pattern:

1. Claim or topic sentence.
2. Evidence or example.
3. Explanation of how the evidence supports the claim.
4. Transition to the next point.

Flag evidence problems when a claim lacks support, the evidence is too general, the source relationship is unclear, or the paragraph ends immediately after a quotation/statistic without analysis.

## Common Academic Organization Patterns

- Process or chronological: steps, stages, methods, historical development.
- Cause/effect: separate causes from effects unless using a chain structure where each event causes the next.
- Comparison/contrast: use point-by-point organization for analytic comparison; use block organization when discussing two subjects separately improves clarity.
- Argumentative: state the position clearly, support it with reasons and evidence, acknowledge counterarguments when relevant, and answer them fairly.

## Revision Sequence

Use this order for substantial polishing:

1. Narrow broad or unfocused claims.
2. Clarify thesis and topic sentences.
3. Remove off-topic sentences or split overloaded paragraphs.
4. Reorder sentences for logic.
5. Add or refine transitions.
6. Improve sentence structure and word choice.
7. Correct punctuation and mechanics.
