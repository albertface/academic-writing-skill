# Sentence Style And Grammar

Use this reference for sentence-level academic polishing.

## Academic Sentence Style

Prefer sentences that are clear, precise, and varied.

- Mix simple, compound, complex, and compound-complex sentences to avoid monotony.
- Use complex sentences to show logical relationships such as cause, contrast, condition, concession, purpose, and time.
- Keep the main claim in the independent clause when possible.
- Avoid needless nominalization when a direct verb is clearer, but keep discipline-standard noun phrases.
- Remove wordiness that does not add scope, precision, or evidential caution.

## Parallel Structure

Maintain parallel grammar in lists, comparisons, headings, and paired constructions.

Check especially:

- X, Y, and Z lists;
- not only ... but also;
- either ... or / neither ... nor;
- both ... and;
- compared with / in contrast to structures;
- repeated methods, results, or contribution statements.

When revising, align grammatical form: noun with noun, verb phrase with verb phrase, clause with clause.

## Sentence Problems

Fix these directly unless doing so risks changing meaning:

- Fragment: a dependent clause or phrase punctuated as a full sentence.
- Run-on sentence: independent clauses joined without proper punctuation or connector.
- Comma splice: independent clauses joined only by a comma.
- Dangling or unclear modifier: introductory phrase does not logically modify the subject that follows.
- Faulty pronoun reference: this, it, they, which, or these lacks a clear antecedent.
- Subject-verb disagreement, tense inconsistency, and article errors.

## Clauses And Logical Relations

Use clause structure to make relationships explicit.

- Noun clauses can report claims, findings, perceptions, and conclusions: "The results suggest that..."
- Adverb clauses can express time, cause, result, purpose, contrast, concession, and condition.
- Adjective clauses can define or add information about key nouns; distinguish restrictive from nonrestrictive clauses.
- Participial phrases can condense prose, but avoid them if the actor becomes ambiguous.

## Punctuation Priorities

- Use commas after introductory dependent clauses.
- Use semicolons only between closely related independent clauses or in complex lists.
- Use commas around nonrestrictive clauses and phrases.
- Avoid comma use that separates a subject from its verb or a verb from its object.
- Preserve citation punctuation unless the target style clearly requires a correction.

## Tone And Word Choice

Academic prose should be formal without becoming inflated.

- Replace conversational wording with precise academic phrasing.
- Avoid exaggeration: "proves," "clearly demonstrates," and "undeniably" often overstate evidence.
- Use cautious verbs when evidence warrants: suggests, indicates, may, can, appears to, is associated with.
- Avoid vague evaluative adjectives unless defined by criteria: good, bad, significant, important, interesting.
- Prefer concrete agents and actions when the method or argument benefits from clarity.

## Editing Checklist

Before returning polished text, check:

- sentence boundaries;
- subject-verb agreement;
- article and plural consistency;
- tense consistency;
- parallel lists;
- clear pronoun reference;
- modifier placement;
- transition accuracy;
- unnecessary repetition;
- preservation of technical meaning.
